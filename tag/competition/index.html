Monopolistically Skewed Business Cycles: On the Origins of Distributional Fluctuations
How do aggregate shocks jointly move mean growth, dispersion, and skewness over the business cycle? In U.S. public-firm data, these three moments fluctuate in stable patterns, yet with cross-sectional heterogeneity: larger firms have lower, more stable growth dispersion but substantially larger skewness fluctuations. We explain these cyclical dynamics and their size gradient through heterogeneous exposure and demand curvature. In general homothetic demand systems, a strengthened form of Marshall’s second law makes firms’ equilibrium policies from cost shocks to log real revenue concave, generating skewed growth. Under mild conditions, larger firms have flatter policies with stronger curvature, rationalizing the size gradient. We confirm this empirically by jointly recovering relative policy slopes, curvature, and idiosyncratic volatility across firm sizes from growth variance and skewness dynamics. Firm-specific responses to six independently identified aggregate innovations broadly reproduce the size gradient in growth moments. Using measured firm-level COVID exposures, we further link aggregate shocks to distributional growth asymmetry.
Frederik Bennhoff, Timo Haber, Niklas Schmitz
